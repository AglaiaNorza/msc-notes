\chapter{Satisfiability in Formal Methods}

\section{The SAT Decision Problem}

\begin{defframe}{The SAT decision problem}{}
    Given a propositional formula in conjunctive normal form, is there a truth assignment to the Boolean variables such that it satisfies all clauses?
\end{defframe}

SAT is an \textit{NP-complete} problem, and it's a very active research area.

\section{Formula transformation}

We have that
\begin{itemize}
    \item every formula can be transformed into an \textbf{equivalent} CNF formula with, in the worst case, an exponential increase in size
    \item every formula can be transformed into an \textbf{equisatisfiable} formula with a \textit{linear} increase in size (by introducing new variables for subformulae)
\end{itemize}

\begin{cframe}{ColorLemma}{Tseitin's transformation}
    \vspace{-1em}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item introduce a fresh boolean variable for every connective
    \item encode each equivalence as a set of clauses
    \item collect all constraints in a big conjunction and enforce the variable representing the entire formula to be true
\end{enumerate}
\end{cframe}

\paragraph{Example.}
Consider the following propositional formula:
\begin{equation*}
\Phi = (a \land (a \lor b)) \lor \neg(a \lor b)
\end{equation*}
First, we break the formula down into an Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) and identify every unique operation, and we then assign a new variable to each (non-atomic) subformula:


\noindent
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{align*}
t_0 &\leftrightarrow (t_1 \lor t_3) \\
t_1 &\leftrightarrow (a \land t_2) \\
t_2 &\leftrightarrow (a \lor b) \\
t_3 &\leftrightarrow \neg t_4 \\
t_4 &\leftrightarrow (a \lor b) 
\end{align*}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    scale=0.75, transform shape,
    level distance=1.2cm,
    level 1/.style={sibling distance=3.5cm},
    level 2/.style={sibling distance=1.5cm},
    level 3/.style={sibling distance=1.5cm},
    every node/.style={align=center}
  ]
  
  \node {$\lor \quad (t_0)$}
    child {node {$\land \quad (t_1)$}
      child {node {$a$}}
      child {node {$\lor \quad (t_2)$}
        child {node {$a$}}
        child {node {$b$}}
      }
    }
    child {node {$\neg \quad (t_3)$}
      child {node {$\lor \quad (t_4)$}
        child {node {$a$}}
        child {node {$b$}}
      }
    };
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{0.5em}
We translate each of these equivalences into standard CNF clauses via:
\begin{itemize}
    \item AND $X \leftrightarrow (Y \land Z)$: \\
    $\equiv (\neg X \lor Y) \land (\neg X \lor Z) \land (X \lor \neg Y \lor \neg Z)$
    
    \item OR $X \leftrightarrow (Y \lor Z)$: \\
    $\equiv (\neg X \lor Y \lor Z) \land (X \lor \neg Y) \land (X \lor \neg Z)$
    
    \item NOT $X \leftrightarrow \neg Y$: \\
    $\equiv (\neg X \lor \neg Y) \land (X \lor Y)$
\end{itemize}

Finally, to assert that the entire formula $\Phi$ is true, we assert the root variable ($t_0$) as a unit clause, and join it with the CNF encodings of all the subformulae:
\begin{align*}
CNF(\Phi) =\; & t_0 \land (t_0 \leftrightarrow (t_1 \lor t_3)) \land (t_1 \leftrightarrow (a \land t_2)) \land (t_2 \leftrightarrow (a \lor b)) \land \\
& (t_3 \leftrightarrow \neg t_4) \land (t_4 \leftrightarrow (a \lor b))
\end{align*}

We get the fully expanded CNF formula:
\begin{align*}
CNF(\Phi) =\; & t_0 \land \\
& (\neg t_0 \lor t_1 \lor t_3) \land (t_0 \lor \neg t_1) \land (t_0 \lor \neg t_3) \land \\
& (\neg t_1 \lor a) \land (\neg t_1 \lor t_2) \land (t_1 \lor \neg a \lor \neg t_2) \land \\
& (\neg t_2 \lor a \lor b) \land (t_2 \lor \neg a) \land (t_2 \lor \neg b) \land \\
& (\neg t_3 \lor \neg t_4) \land (t_3 \lor t_4) \land \\
& (\neg t_4 \lor a \lor b) \land (t_4 \lor \neg a) \land (t_4 \lor \neg b)
\end{align*}

This resulting formula is equisatisfiable to the original $\Phi$.


\begin{cframe}{ColorThm}{Plasted-Greenbaum's transformation}
 Every direction in the Tseitin encoding can be replaced by $\to $ or $\leftarrow $, where the direction of the implication depends on the \textit{polarity} of the formula
        \begin{itemize}
            \item for every occurrence of a subformula $\varphi$, the polarity is $+$ if the number of negations above $\varphi$ in the syntax tree is even, and $-$ otherwise
            \item we add $t_{i} \to $ if the polarity of $t_{i}$ is positive, and $\leftarrow t_{i}$ otherwise
            \item so, every $\to $ and $\leftarrow $ can be replaced by at most two clauses
        \end{itemize}
\end{cframe}

\section{Resolution}
The theory underlying SAT solving is propositional resolution.

\begin{defframe}{Resolution}{}
The resolution rule is:
\vspace{-1em}
\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$C \cup \{v\}$}
    \AxiomC{$D \cup \{ \neg  v\}$}
    \BinaryInfC{$C \cup D$}
\end{prooftree}
Which tells us that resolving the clause $C \cup \{v\}$ with the clause $D \cup \{\neg v\}$ results in the resolvant clause $D \cup C$.

We also have a \textbf{unit-resolution} rule (or \textbf{BCP}, boolean constraint propagation). This rule can be used on so-called \textit{unit clauses}, clauses with a single literal, which needs to be assigned to $\top$ in a CNF.

\begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{$C \cup \{\neg v\}$}
    \AxiomC{$\{v\}$}
    \RightLabel{\quad $\{I, \neg I\} \cap C = \emptyset$}
    \BinaryInfC{$C$}
\end{prooftree}
\begin{gframe}{NB!!}
    This rule is \textit{not refutation-complete}, but  it can be applied in time \textit{linear} to the size of the formula.
\end{gframe}
\end{defframe}

With the unit-resolution rule, we can do \textbf{Unit Propagation}: when a unit resolution produces a unit clause, the unit-resolution step can be repeated with the new clause (until fixpoint).

\begin{cframe}{ColorLemma}{BCP algorithm}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item find a unit clause $I$
        \item remove all the clauses in which $I$ occurs
        \item remove all literals $\neg I$ in clauses
        \item repeat
    \end{itemize}
\end{cframe}

\paragraph{Example}
We want to check the validity of:
$$a \land b \to (a \leftrightarrow b)$$

To check validity, we must check unsatisfiability of the negated formula.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item CNF of negated formula:
    \begin{align*}
    & \neg(a \land b \to (a \leftrightarrow b)) \\
    \equiv\ & a \land b \land \neg(a \leftrightarrow b) \\
    \equiv\ & a \land b \land \neg((\neg a \lor b) \land (a \lor \neg b)) \\
    \equiv\ & a \land b \land (\neg(\neg a \lor b) \lor \neg(a \lor \neg b)) \\
    \equiv\ & a \land b \land ((a \land \neg b) \lor (\neg a \land b)) \\
    \equiv\ & a \land b \land (a \lor \neg a) \land (a \lor b) \land (\neg b \lor \neg a) \land (\neg b \lor b) \\
    \equiv\ & a \land b \land (a \lor b) \land (\neg a \lor \neg b)
    \end{align*}

    \item Unit resolution on $a$ with $(\neg a \lor \neg b)$ \\
    $$a \land b \land (a \lor b) \land (\neg a \lor \neg b) \land (\neg b)$$

    \item Unit propagation:
    $$b \land (\neg b)$$

    \item Unit resolution on $b$ with $(\neg b)$ \\
        $$\text{empty clause}\bot$$
\end{enumerate}
The negation is UNSAT, therefore the original formula is valid.

\section{The DPLL Algorithm}
The DPLL (Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland) algorithm followed the DP (Davis-Putnam) algorithm. Where the first eliminated variables via full resolution, causing an explosion in memory, DPLL branches on variable values, trading memory for execution time (DPLL runs in polynomial space, at the cost of a worst case exponential runtime).


\begin{cframe}{ColorLemma}{DPLL algorithm}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item apply repeated unit resolution and removal of subsumed clauses
            \subitem \textcolor{gray}{at this point, any literals have been removed and the other clauses have been ``cleaned up''}
        \item if $\emptyset \in F$, return UNSAT
        \item if $F = \emptyset$ (no clauses left), return SAT
        \item choose a new variable $x$ (using a decision heuristic), and branch on it:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item add $\{x\}$ and recursively solve
                \item add $\{\neg x\}$ and recursively solve
            \end{itemize}
        \item if one of the branches returns SAT, return SAT
        \item otherwise, return UNSAT
    \end{itemize}

\end{cframe}

There is an optional optimisation that can be added (but that could be too expensive compared to simple BCP):
\begin{gframe}{Pure Literal Rule}
 If a variable appears only with positive polarity (or only negative polarity) throughout $F$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item setting that literal to $\top$ cannot falsify any clause
    \item all clauses containing that pure literal can be eliminated immediately
\end{itemize}
\end{gframe}

\paragraph{Kinds of assignments in DPLL:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decided ($\bigwedge$):} arbitrary choice/guess when BCP cannot proceed
    \item \textbf{Propagated ($|$):} values logically forced by unit resolution (BCP) until $\bot$ or a stall
\end{itemize}

The main drawback of the DPLL algorithm is its \textbf{blindness to found conflicts}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item  when a conflict ($\bot$) is encountered, DPLL mechanically undoes only the most recent decision and tries the opposite branch, without analyzing which decisions actually caused the conflict (\textit{chronological backtracking})
    \item if a conflict is caused by an independent subset of variables, changing the assignment of an unrelated higher-level variable (like $c$) forces DPLL to rediscover and prove the exact same conflict all over again
    \item DPLL does not remember or record why a dead end was reached; it possesses no memory across distinct branches of the search tree
\end{itemize}

\section{Clause-Driven Conflict Learning}
Clause-Driven Conflict Learning improves on DPLL by implementing \textbf{backjumping}, a technique that helps to recover from bad decisions by skipping redundant conflicts.

To implement backjumping, one needs to recognise, store and update \textit{which decisions} contributed to conflicts. CDCL solvers achieve this through \textbf{Conflict Analysis} and \textbf{Clause Learning}.

\subsection{Implication Graph}
An \textbf{implication graph} is a labelled DAG $G = (V, E)$ where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V$ represents the literals of the current partial assignment (\textit{trail})
    \subitem nodes are labelled with their corresponding literal and decision level ($x = v @ d$ where $v \in \{0,1\}$)
    \item $E$ represents the reasons of the assignments
    \subitem edges are labelled with the reason clause used for propagation
\item there is a special node $k$ representing a conflict - it has no outgoing edges, and its incoming edges are:
    $$\{(l, k) | \neg l \in C, C \in F\}$$
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $l$ is a literal node in the implication graph that has been assigned true
        \item $C \in F$ is the clause that caused the conflict (i.e. has been falsified)
        \item $\neg l \in C$ is the qualifying condition for the edge: the negation of $l$ is a component of the clause $C$ ($\neg l$ is false, and this falsification contributes to breaking clause $C$)
        \item $(l,k)$ a directed edge is drawn from the node $l$ to the conflict node $k$, labeled with $C$ to track which clause caused the conflict
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{gframe}{Example}
Suppose the formula contains the clause $C = (\neg x_1 \lor x_2 \lor \neg x_3)$.

For $C$ to become a conflicting (falsified) clause, the current decision trail must contain the assignments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_1 = \text{true}$ (node $x_1$ exists in the graph)
    \item $x_2 = \text{false}$ (node $\neg x_2$ exists in the graph)
    \item $x_3 = \text{true}$ (node $x_3$ exists in the graph)
\end{itemize}

The nodes responsible for the conflict are $x_1$, $\neg x_2$, and $x_3$. Therefore, the incoming edges to the conflict node $\kappa$ will originate from $x_1$, $\neg x_2$, and $x_3$, representing the assignments that caused $C$ to fail.
\end{gframe}

\paragraph{Implication Graph Example.}
Let the formula $F$ be:
\[ F = C_1 \land C_2 \land C_3 = (\neg a \lor \neg b \lor c) \land (\neg b \lor \neg c) \land (\neg a \lor b) \]

Suppose our first decision is to assign $a$ to true (at decision level 1). This triggers Boolean Constraint Propagation (BCP) as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $a = 1$ forces $b = 1$ to satisfy $C_3$
    \item $b = 1$ forces $c = 0$ to satisfy $C_2$
    \item now $a = 1$, $b = 1$, and $c = 0$, which falsifies clause $C_1$
\end{enumerate}

\begin{gframe}{Simple implementation details}
In modern CDCL solvers, the implication graph is not stored as an explicit graph data structure. Instead, it is implicitly maintained via the assignment trail (stack of currently assigned variables).

For each assigned variable, we store a tuple \texttt{(Value, Decision Level, Reason Pointer)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{reason pointer (antecedent):} pointer to the clause that forced this assignment
    \subitem storing this pointer allows the solver to instantly trace backward from a conflict to compute a learned clause via resolution, without re-scanning the clause database
    \item \textbf{decision variables:} variables chosen by the decision heuristic (guesses) are not forced by any clause. Their reason pointer is set to \texttt{null} (so,during conflict analysis, a \texttt{null} pointer signifies a root node in the implication graph)
\end{itemize}
\end{gframe}

Implication graphs allow us to trace the causes of variable assignments and conflicts.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Finding the cause of an assignment (the antecedent):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item locate the node $n$ whose assignment you want to analyze
        \item trace its incoming edges (look at the arrows pointing into $n$); let's say they come from nodes $a$, $b$, and $c$
        \item reconstruct the underlying clause: the edges imply that $a \land b \land c \to n$; converting this implication into standard CNF format gives us: $\neg a \lor \neg b \lor \neg c \lor n$
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Finding the conflicting clause for a conflict node ($\kappa$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the same logic applies, but the implication has no consequent (it implies a contradiction/false)
        \item trace the incoming edges to $\kappa$ (e.g., from nodes $x$ and $y$)
        \item simply negate these parent nodes and connect them with disjunctions; the clause that caused the conflict is $\neg x \lor \neg y$
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\end{cframe}

\subsection{Clause Learning}
To help us decide where to backjump to, and to learn more from a found conflict, we can add \textbf{clause learning} to our algorithm.

Whenever we encounter a conflict clause, we can add it to the formula: the literals contributing to a conflict form a partial assignment, which is just a conjunction of literals, whose negation is a clause that is \textit{implied by the original CNF}. Therefore, adding this clause prevents the partial assignment from happening again during the search.

\begin{gframe}{Finding cuts in the graph}
    A visual way to see this is to imagine it as finding cuts in the implication graph. We can draw a \textbf{cut} through the implication graph that separates the decision variables (roots) from the conflict node $\kappa$ (leaf).

However, not all cuts are useful. There are two extremes we want to avoid:

\begin{itemize}
\item the decision cut (closest to roots)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item  results in a disjunction of all the negated decisions that led to the conflict
        \item it is usually very long and provides almost no new information: it essentially just tells the solver, ``don't make this exact combination of guesses again,'' which does not help prune the remaining search space efficiently
    \end{itemize}

    \item the conflict cut (closest to leaf):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item results in the exact clause that was just falsified to cause the conflict
        \item gives no new information: the solver already has this clause in its original formula database
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Because the decision cut is too broad (weak) and the conflict cut is redundant, modern CDCL solvers look for a cut in the middle of the graph. The goal is to find a cut that produces a \textbf{short} clause (because shorter clauses restrict the search space more heavily) and contains \textbf{novel information} not explicitly written in the original formula. 
\end{gframe}

\subsubsection{Unique Implication Points (UIPs) and Asserting Clauses}

To find the optimal cut in an implication graph, modern solvers rely on finding a "bottleneck" that forces the conflict. This bottleneck is called a Unique Implication Point.

\begin{defframe}{Unique Implication Point (UIP)}{}
 A node in the implication graph at the \textit{current} decision level $d$ is a UIP if \textit{every path} from the decision variable at level $d$ to the conflict node $\kappa$ must pass through it.
\begin{itemize}
    \item in graph theory, a UIP is known as a \textbf{dominator} of the conflict node with respect to the decision node
    \item (it represents a single, unavoidable consequence at the current decision level that triggered the conflict; if you remove the UIP, the conflict cannot happen)
\end{itemize}
        \begin{gframe}{Nota Bene}
The decision variable itself is \textit{always} trivially a UIP, because all paths at the current level originate from it.
        \end{gframe}
\end{defframe}

Because there can be multiple UIPs in a single implication graph (e.g., the decision variable, and several intermediate forced variables), we must choose for our cut.

The best one to choose is almost always the \textbf{First UIP}, i.e. the one \textit{closest to the conflict node} $k$.

\begin{gframe}{Why the First UIP?}
        \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Locality:} it captures the most recent, localized reasons for the conflict, making the resulting learned clause highly relevant to the current search space
        \item \textit{Efficiency:} the algorithm to find the 1UIP works backward from the conflict; stopping at the \textit{first} UIP we encounter is computationally faster than resolving all the way back to the decision variable
    \end{itemize}
\end{gframe}

When we perform resolution backward from the conflict and stop at the First UIP, the resulting learned clause is called an \textbf{Asserting Clause}. 

\begin{defframe}{Asserting Clause}{}
 A conflict clause that contains \textit{exactly one literal from the current decision level} (which is the negation of the UIP node). All other literals in the clause come from earlier, lower decision levels.
\end{defframe}

    This structural property of the asserting clause makes non-chronological backjumping work perfectly:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item when we learn an asserting clause, we look at the decision levels of its literals and backjump to the \textbf{second-highest decision level} present in the clause
        \item because the clause only had \textit{one} literal from the highest level, backjumping clears that assignment
        \item upon arriving at the second-highest level, \textbf{every literal in the learned clause evaluates to False, except for one} (the UIP literal, which is now unassigned)
        \item this immediately triggers Boolean Constraint Propagation; the learned clause \textit{asserts} the forced assignment of the UIP literal, driving the solver forward without needing a new blind guess
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection*{Detection of UIPs via Resolution}

To compute the First UIP clause (the asserting clause), the solver iteratively computes (by resolution) the clauses that are associated with cuts moving \textit{backward} from the conflict.

\begin{cframe}{ColorLemma}{UIP Detection Algorithm}
    \vspace{-1em}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item let $c_0$ be the first conflict clause
    \item pick the literal $\ell \in c_0$ that was \textbf{last assigned} on the trail
    \item \textbf{while} there is more than one literal in $c_i$ that is assigned at the \textit{current} decision level:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item let $c_{i+1}$ be the resolvent of $c_i$ and the \textit{reason clause} of $\ell$ (apply a resolution step to $c_{i}$ and the reason clause of $\ell$)
        \item let $\ell$ be the literal $\ell' \in c_{i+1}$ that was last assigned
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{cframe}

\begin{itemize}

    \item The literal $\ell$ in our conflict clause is false (which contributed to the conflict). Because $\ell$ was forced via BCP, it has a \textit{reason clause} that caused its assignment.
    \item By resolving the current conflict clause $C_i$ with the reason clause of $\ell$, we eliminate $\ell$ (and $\neg \ell$) and merge the remaining literals. We have effectively taken a step backward in the implication graph, moving the ``cut'' behind $\ell$ (every intermediate generated clause $C_i$ corresponds to a valid cut in the implication graph). To ensure we move backward through the graph without skipping or getting tangled, we resolve variables in the exact reverse order of how they were assigned.
\item The loop stops exactly when $C_i$ contains only \textit{one} literal from the current decision level. The last $C_i$ produced is the \textbf{First UIP-clause}
    \item As we established, because this final clause contains exactly one literal from the highest decision level, it is an asserting clause
\end{itemize}

\begin{gframe}{First UIP Example}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{conflict node ($\kappa$):} falsified by assignments $y=1@4$ and $z=1@4$.
    \item \textbf{initial conflicting clause ($c_0$):} $(\neg y \lor \neg z)$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{resolution trace:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{1st resolution (resolving $y$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the literal $y$ was forced by $h, i$ (from level 2) and $t$ (from level 4). 
        \item \textbf{reason for $y$:} $(\neg h \lor \neg i \lor \neg t \lor y)$
        \item \textbf{resolvent ($c_1$):} resolve $c_0$ with reason($y$) to eliminate $y$.
        \item $c_1 = (\neg h \lor \neg i \lor \neg t \lor \neg z)$
        \item \textit{level 4 literals remaining:} $t$ and $z$. (more than one, so we continue).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{2nd resolution (resolving $t$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the literal $t$ was forced by $d$ (level 1), $g$ (level 2), and $s$ (level 4).
        \item \textbf{reason for $t$:} $(\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor t)$
        \item \textbf{resolvent ($c_2$):} resolve $c_1$ with reason($t$) to eliminate $t$.
        \item $c_2 = (\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor \neg h \lor \neg i \lor \neg z)$
        \item \textit{level 4 literals remaining:} $s$ and $z$.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{3rd resolution (resolving $z$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the literal $z$ was forced purely by $x$ (level 4).
        \item \textbf{reason for $z$:} $(\neg x \lor z)$
        \item \textbf{resolvent ($c_3$):} resolve $c_2$ with reason($z$) to eliminate $z$.
        \item $c_3 = (\neg x \lor \neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor \neg h \lor \neg i)$
        \item \textit{level 4 literals remaining:} $x$ and $s$.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{4th resolution (resolving $x$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the literal $x$ was forced by $s$ (level 4).
        \item \textbf{reason for $x$:} $(\neg s \lor x)$
        \item \textbf{resolvent ($c_4$):} resolve $c_3$ with reason($x$) to eliminate $x$. 
        \item \textit{note:} because $\neg s$ is already present in $c_3$, merging them performs \textit{self-subsuming resolution} (the duplicate simply absorbs).
        \item $c_4 = (\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor \neg h \lor \neg i)$
        \item \textit{level 4 literals remaining:} \textbf{only $s$!}
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{conclusion:}
$c_4$ now contains exactly one literal ($\neg s$) from the highest decision level (level 4), so the algorithm terminates. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{first UIP:} the variable $s$.
    \item \textbf{the learned (asserting) clause:} $(\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor \neg h \lor \neg i)$. 
    \item \textbf{backjumping execution:} the solver looks at the variables in the learned clause ($s@4$, $d@1$, $g@2$, $h@2$, $i@2$); the second-highest decision level is \textbf{level 2}, so the solver will backjump to it, clear all level 3 and 4 assignments, and the new learned clause will immediately force $s$ to be false via BCP.
\end{itemize}
\end{gframe}

\subsection*{Minimizing the 1st UIP Clause}
While the asserting clause is good enough, it can often be shrunk further. Shorter clauses often preferred, as they restrict the search space more effectively. Modern solvers use resolution techniques to analyze the implication graph and prune redundant literals from the learned clause.

there are two levels of minimization: \textbf{local} and \textbf{recursive}.




\begin{cframe}{ColorLemma}{Local Minimization}
Local minimization looks at the \textit{immediate} antecedents (parents) of the literals in the learned clause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item If all the immediate antecedents of a literal $\ell$ are \textit{already present} (marked) in the learned clause, then $\ell$ is redundant and can be removed.
    \item This is an application of \textit{self-subsuming resolution}. If our learned clause contains $(\dots \lor \neg h \lor \neg i)$, and the reason $i$ was forced in the first place was $h$ (meaning the reason clause is $\neg h \lor i$), we can resolve them. Resolving the learned clause with the reason clause eliminates $i$ and leaves us with $(\dots \lor \neg h \lor \neg h)$, which simplifies to just $(\dots \lor \neg h)$.
\subitem (if $i$ was only forced to be true because $h$ was true (reason clause: $\neg h \lor i$), then $h$ being true automatically drags $i$ along with it; therefore, assigning $h = \text{true}$ is enough to guarantee the conflict by itself, and we do not need to explicitly ban $i$: banning $h$ covers the root cause)
\subitem (we resolve the learned clause $(\dots \lor \neg h \lor \neg i)$ with the reason clause $(\neg h \lor i)$, which yields $(\dots \lor \neg h)$, safely eliminating the redundant variable $i$)
        \vspace{-1em}
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Algorithm:}

    \begin{enumerate}
\vspace{-1em}
        \item mark all variables present in the first UIP clause
        \item for each literal, check its reason clause
        \item if every other literal in the reason clause is also marked, remove the candidate literal
    \end{enumerate}
\end{cframe}

\begin{cframe}{ColorThm}{Recursive Minimization}
Local minimization is fast but misses deeper redundancies. What if a literal's antecedent is \textit{not} in the learned clause, but that antecedent was forced by things that \textit{are}? Recursive minimization traces paths all the way back through the graph.

\begin{itemize}
    \item a candidate literal can be removed if \textit{every path} backward from its antecedents in the implication graph eventually terminates at either:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item a literal that is already marked (present in the learned clause)
        \item a top-level assignment (decision level 0), because level 0 variables are universally true
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

    \vspace{-1.5em}
\paragraph{Algorithm:}
    \begin{enumerate}
\vspace{-1em}
        \item mark all variables in the 1st UIP clause
        \item for each candidate literal, DFS backward through its antecedents in the implication graph
        \item if the search always hits marked literals or level 0 assignments, the candidate is redundant and is removed
    \end{enumerate}
\begin{itemize}
    \item To keep this fast, if the backward search ever hits an unmarked decision variable, or pulls in a variable from a different decision level (that isn't level 0), the search for that candidate terminates early with a failure (the candidate cannot be removed).
\end{itemize}
\end{cframe}

\begin{gframe}{Example}
    Consider the first UIP:
 $$(\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor \neg h \lor \neg i)$$
 \begin{itemize}
\item \textit{local minimization:} $i$ is removed because its only antecedent is $h$, which is already in the clause; the clause becomes $(\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s \lor \neg h)$.
    \item \textit{recursive minimization:} $h$ is investigated; its antecedents are $g$ (which is marked) and $e$ (unmarked)
        \subitem the search recursively checks $e$: the antecedents of $e$ are $d$ (marked) and $b$ (Level 0); because all paths hit marked or Level 0 nodes, $h$ is also safely removed
    \item \textit{minimized clause:} $(\neg d \lor \neg g \lor \neg s)$
\end{itemize}
\end{gframe}

\subsection{Clause Database Reduction Strategies}
While learning clauses is essential for pruning the search space, keeping \textit{all} learned clauses forever is detrimental.

The main reasons why periodically cleaning out the clause database is beneficial are intuitive:
\begin{itemize}
    \item too many clauses will eventually exhaust system memory
    \item the more clauses the solver has to watch and evaluate, the slower BCP becomes
    \item as the solver moves to different areas of the search space, older learned clauses often become irrelevant to the current search focus
    \item some clauses become permanently useless (e.g., if a clause is satisfied by a top-level assignment, it will never be falsified again)
\end{itemize}

Commonly-used heuristics include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Size Heuristics:} shorter clauses are more powerful, so long clauses get discarded
    \item \textit{Least Recently Used (LRU):} the solver tracks when a clause was last used in BCP or conflict analysis and deletes those that haven't been used recently (like in cache design)
    \item \textit{Clause Activity Scores:} clauses can be given a score that is bumped every time they participate in generating a new conflict clause; low-scoring clauses are discarded
    \item \textbf{Glucose Level / LBD (Literal Block Distance)} (very common):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item based on the number of \textit{distinct decision levels} present in the literals in the learned clause
        \item a clause might be long, but if all its variables were assigned at the same decision level, they are tightly clustered in the search tree; a low LBD means the clause acts like a short clause
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Decision Heuristics}
Another important aspect is that of choosing which unassigned variable BCP should choose next.

A good heuristic should be fast to compute, it should lead the solver to conflicts quickly, and it should be adaptable to the solver's current focus (e.g. involving variables involved in recent conflicts).

\begin{cframe}{ColorLemma}{VSIDS: Variable State Independent Decaying Sum}
VSIDS is the standard heuristic used in almost all modern CDCL solvers. It scores variables based on their involvement in recent conflicts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item every variable starts with a score (often 0, or based on its initial frequency in the formula)
    \item whenever a conflict occurs and a new clause is learned, the solver increases the score of all variables involved in the conflict analysis (e.g., variables in the learned clause and the paths resolving to it)
    \item periodically, the solver divides the scores of \textit{all} variables in the system by a constant factor (so that the numbers don't take up too much space in memory / don't overflow)
\end{itemize}
When a decision has to be made, the solver simply picks the unassigned variable with the highest current score
\end{cframe}

\subsection{Restarts}
In SAT solving, algorithms often suffer from "heavy-tail behavior.": due to a few bad early decisions, the solver might wander into a massive, heavily constrained subtree of the search space that takes too long to evaluate, even though a satisfying assignment (or a quick proof of unsatisfiability) might exist in a different branch.

To escape these dead ends, solvers frequently \textbf{restart} the search process.
\begin{itemize}
    \item the solver abandons its current assignment trail and jumps completely back to Decision Level 0.
    \item but it doesn't reset its state completely: it keeps all the learned clauses, and the current VSIDS variable scores
\end{itemize}

Since the solver retains its learned clauses and heuristic scores, the next search will not be identical to the previous one:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the VSIDS scores have been altered by the conflicts encountered during the abandoned run, meaning the solver will choose variables in a different order
    \item the newly learned clauses will act as roadblocks, steering the solver away from the bad paths that caused the previous conflicts
\end{itemize}

Restarts are scheduled according to various mathematical sequences to balance short, aggressive searches with deeper explorations.
